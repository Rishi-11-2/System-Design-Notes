Critical mental models and non-negotiable principles for engineering microservices: managing distributed tech debt, resolving team friction, avoiding resume-driven development, and aligning architectural complexity with business ROI.

1. Architecture vs. Business ROI: The Pragmatic Lens

Microservices are frequently adopted for the wrong reasons. Engineers often champion microservices because they are fashionable, exciting to build, or represent an opportunity to master modern cloud-native tools like Kubernetes, Kafka, and Envoy.

However, software architecture does not exist to satisfy engineering curiosity; it exists to deliver business value. An architecture that increases operational downtime, inflates cloud infrastructure bills, and slows down feature delivery is a failed architecture, regardless of how elegantly its microservices are decoupled.

The Fundamental Question

Does breaking this capability into an out-of-process network service increase the organization's business velocity, or does it merely introduce distributed latency, eventual consistency headaches, and multi-repository maintenance overhead?

2. The Growth Opportunity & 'Resume-Driven' Trap

In growing companies, microservices are often created as an organizational mechanism to give junior or mid-level engineers "ownership" of a service. While empowering, creating 20 microservices for a 15-person engineering department produces an untenable ratio of services to developers.

When an engineer leaves the company, who maintains the 4 obscure services they built in a niche framework? The organization suffers orphaned services, abandoned dependency updates, and recurring production brownouts.

3. Managing Cross-Team Conflicts & API Boundaries

In a monolithic architecture, disagreements over code organization and database schema design manifest as pull request debates. In microservices, team conflicts manifest as API boundary friction:

  • Passing the Blame: When an edge request fails, Service A blames Service B's latency, while Service B blames Service C's database locks. Distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry) is required to establish indisputable root cause telemetry.
  • The Consumer-Supplier Dynamic: Upstream services must treat downstream internal squads as paying customers. Breaking changes must never be deployed unilaterally; upstream teams must support semantic versioning and backward compatibility.

6. The 7 Non-Negotiable Microservice Axioms

  1. Model Around Business Concepts, Not Technology: Group services by domain capabilities (Orders, Shipping), never by tech layers (Postgres service, Redis service).
  2. Hide Internal Implementation Details: Never expose database IDs or storage models directly in public API contracts.
  3. Decentralize All The Things: Decentralize governance and data; avoid centralized orchestrator bottlenecks where possible.
  4. Deploy Independently: If Service A cannot be deployed without simultaneously deploying Service B, they are a distributed monolith. Merge them.
  5. Isolate Failure Domains: Design every network hop with timeouts, bulkheads, and circuit breakers.
  6. Observe Everything: A service without metrics, health probes, and distributed tracing is unfit for production.
  7. Culture of Automation: Microservices at scale require automated testing, automated CI/CD, and infrastructure as code.

7. Production Engineering Checklist

  • Service Catalog & Ownership: Maintain a live catalog (e.g. Backstage) documenting the owning on-call team for every microservice.
  • Deprecation Protocols: Establish clear SLAs for retiring legacy API versions (minimum 6 months deprecation notice).
  • Deadlock Linting: Map inter-service dependency graphs and flag any bidirectional or cyclic dependencies.
  • Business Case Sign-Off: Require architecture review approval demonstrating measurable business ROI before creating a new service.
